Foreword · Of Fire & Salt

Between the two languages — Türkçe and the older language of fire — there is one shared word: destân. To carry a saga forward, the storyteller sat close to the flame and spoke until the embers cooled. So it goes still, in a kitchen on a quiet Toronto street, where the cooks turn the roast slowly and the room learns the story by its smell.

What follows is not a menu. It is a reel — fifteen plates, a foreword, three chapters, a closing colophon — composed for Destan over the seasons of MMXXVI by FrameFlow. Brand mark, photography, social, and the small bright work of advertising. An epic in a humble form.

Read it as you would a book that someone troubled to bind well. Slowly, with the fire close.

Chapter the First

Of the Fire.

Of cağ kebabı, the rotating roast of Erzurum, and how it must be read at the moment of its darkest crust.

— xii —

I · Of the Fire

The cağ — pronounced djah — was once the food of mountain weddings, the meat marinated three days in onion and salt, then bound horizontally upon the spit and turned, by hand, before a wall of beech embers. It is not doner. The doner stands. The cağ lies down. Where the doner is a tower, the cağ is a story.

At Destan, the spit is fed by oak, and the room is built so that the cook may stand beside the fire and watch it as one watches a long sentence resolve itself. The customer does not order. The customer is brought a plate when the meat is ready to be read.

Where the doner stands, the cağ lies down.

It is, then, an epic in slow motion — and the camera, like the cook, must learn patience. We made this work over four shoots, in light that asked to be waited on.

I · Of the Fire

We learned the cook’s tempo by waiting through it. Three pulls of the lever, one long reach for the long blade, a glance to the doorway where the lamb-salt of the marinade had reached the threshold like an invitation. That, in our language, is the picture: the fire glances back, the cook glances back, the camera glances back — three witnesses to one rotation.

We showed early frames to the chef. He said only: “make it longer.” So we did.

Three witnesses to one rotation.

Chapter the Second

Of the Cut.

Of the long blade, the still hand, and the moment when an epic becomes a portion.

— xviii —

II · Of the Cut

The cut, when it comes, is short. The blade is long enough that one pull can free a whole portion; the wrist must be soft enough that the meat remembers it was once cared for. Between the chef and the spit there is a black glove and a handle worn pale by ten thousand of these movements.

We made six frames of this gesture, then chose three. Plate IV stands in for them all: the blade, the meat, the pale handle, the small line of fat caught in the act of falling.

Four shoots · 412 frames · 15 plates kept.

Chapter the Third

Of the Plate.

Of adana, of ezme, and of the table that holds them.

Plate · XV The Spread

“A table set in Toronto, with the grammar of Anatolia.”

— xxiv —

III · Of the Plate

An adana arrives in two long lines of köfte; ezme arrives in a copper bowl that holds its colour against the wood; lavash arrives folded twice. The plate, like the saga, is composed of pieces that only become themselves in one another’s company.

It is — though we are wary of the word — an argument. That food does not need to shout to be loud; that the heat is not lost when the dish is set down; that a kebab house can still believe in its own myth.
